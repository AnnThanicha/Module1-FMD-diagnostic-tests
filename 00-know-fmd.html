Disease background and introduction to diagnostic tests

Intro

What is foot-and-mouth disease?

Foot-and-mouth disease (FMD) is a severe, highly contagious viral disease of livestock. It has a large economic impact. It disrupts livestock production and regional and international trade, so it is a transboundary animal disease. The cause is an Aphthovirus in the family Picornaviridae.

Hosts
Cattle, pigs, sheep, goats and other cloven-hoofed animals. FMD is not readily transmissible to humans and is not a public health risk.
Virus
Seven serotypes: A, O, C, SAT1, SAT2, SAT3 and Asia1. Serotype C has not been isolated since 2004.
Signs
Fever and blister-like sores on the tongue and lips, in the mouth, on the teats and between the hooves. Incubation is 2–14 days.
Spread
Infected animals, contaminated materials, clothing and equipment, meat products, and air currents. Virus may be in milk and semen up to 4 days before signs appear.
Impact
Morbidity can reach 100% in susceptible herds. Adult mortality is low (1–5%). In young calves, lambs and piglets it is 20% or higher.
Control
Early detection, warning and surveillance, plus vaccination with at least 80% coverage.

Source: WOAH, Foot and mouth disease.

Laboratory diagnosis of any suspected FMD case is a matter of urgency. The WOAH Terrestrial Manual gives these reasons.

Look-alikes
FMD cannot be told apart clinically from swine vesicular disease, vesicular stomatitis or vesicular exanthema.
Variable signs
Signs range from inapparent to severe. They depend on the virus strain, dose, age, breed, species and immunity. Some species, such as African buffalo, may be infected without signs.
Hidden cases
Young animals may die suddenly from myocarditis, and vesicles are not always present. Adults on the same premises may show lesions.
Carriers
Some ruminants carry virus in the throat for more than 28 days after infection. In cattle this usually lasts under 6 months, but in a few it lasts up to 3 years. Pigs do not become carriers.
Many serotypes
Infection with one serotype does not protect against another, so the serotype must be known to choose a matching vaccine.
Biosafety
The virus is highly contagious. Samples go only to authorised laboratories, and virus work needs Containment Group 4 facilities.

Best sample. Epithelium from an unruptured or freshly ruptured vesicle, or vesicular fluid. If none is available, use throat (oesophageal–pharyngeal) fluid from a probang cup in ruminants, a throat swab in pigs, or blood. Source: WOAH Terrestrial Manual, chapter 2.1.5.

0.3 · DIVA

SP and NSP antibodies: telling vaccinated from infected

DIVA means differentiating infected from vaccinated animals. Both kinds of animal can have neutralising antibody in their serum, so a plain antibody test cannot tell them apart. For trade, we need to know which animals were infected and which were only vaccinated.

The idea
The virus particle has structural proteins (SP) that make up its shell. Its non-structural proteins (NSP, such as 2C and 3ABC) are made inside infected cells to copy the virus. Vaccines made from purified, inactivated virus particles give antibody almost only to SP, at least after a small number of vaccinations. Infection gives antibody to both.

Limits of NSP tests
  • NSP antibody lasts a long time, so a positive animal is not necessarily still infected. It may be a carrier.
  • Some persistently infected animals do not make NSP antibody. This can happen in previously vaccinated animals, where virus replicates only a little.
  • No antibody test can separate carriers from other animals that have responded to FMDV.
  • Crude vaccine, made from unpurified cell culture supernatant, contains viral NSP. Vaccinated animals can then make NSP antibody and test NSP-positive.
  • Repeated vaccination adds to this. Antibody to contaminating NSP can build up with each booster, so false NSP positives become more likely.
  • Use NSP results at herd level, with virus detection for confirmation.

Case

FMD returns to Indonesia, 2022

Indonesia was free from FMD for decades. The last case was in December 1983, vaccination stopped after 1985, and WOAH recognised the country as free without vaccination in 1990. On 10 April 2022 the first outbreak was reported in Gresik, East Java. By the end of April, cattle farms in Sidoarjo and Lamongan were also affected. In May, outbreaks with FMD-like signs were reported across Java, Sumatra, Kalimantan and West Nusa Tenggara. Cases peaked in June 2022, fell through the year, and rose slightly again in early 2023. The virus was FMDV serotype O, lineage O/ME-SA/Ind-2001e.

Animals
In Java in 2022 and 2023, 41,129 farms had outbreaks and 308,148 animals were affected. 96.4% were cattle. The farm count fell from 37,266 in 2022 to 3,863 in 2023.
Signs
Fever, loss of appetite, and vesicles in the mouth or between the claws and on the coronary band. Cattle also drooled.
Peak
June 2022, with 19,049 outbreak farms and 157,931 cases in that month.
Response
Epidemic status and movement restrictions in early May 2022. First mass vaccination from late June to July 2022 and a second round in January and February 2023. Endemic status was declared in June 2023.
Why it spread
No immunity in the herds, no vaccine stockpile, little experience with FMD among farmers and animal health workers, and late reporting of suspect cases.
